\begin{abstract}
Skillful subseasonal forecasts of precipitation and near-surface temperature are needed for hydrologic risk, agriculture, energy planning, public health, and disaster preparedness, yet raw dynamical ensembles often retain regional biases and imperfect probabilistic calibration at weeks 1--4. We introduce \methodname{}, a global dynamical-model-informed machine-learning framework that refines \modelname{} (\modelshort{}) subseasonal ensemble guidance into stochastic, spatially coherent ensemble forecasts of weekly precipitation and 2 m temperature. The central design choice is to generate ensemble members from a physically informed stochastic prior rather than from purely isotropic Gaussian noise: initial perturbations combine regime-conditioned EOF structures tied to MJO, NAO, ENSO, and lead-week state with random support and variance-tempered spread control. The implemented system conditions a lead-aware flow-matching U-Net on \modelshort{} ensemble summary fields, recent observed land-ocean-atmosphere predictors, static geography, seasonal phase, and lead time. It predicts precipitation directly and predicts 2 m temperature as a residual relative to the target-lead \modelshort{} ensemble mean. Probabilistic forecasts are generated by integrating the learned velocity field from these structured initial states, so the flow refines physically plausible large-scale covariance patterns rather than building all spatial organization from white noise. In a 2021 full-year noise-prior ablation, the EOF-based higher-spread setting improves all-grid CRPS skill from 24.9\% to 28.4\% for precipitation and from 41.7\% to 43.2\% for 2 m temperature relative to pure Gaussian sampling. The verification design evaluates all-case forecast quality, noise-prior ablations, and extreme-event tail risk using CRPS, RMSE, bias, correlation, Brier Skill Score, calibrated Brier Skill Score, spread diagnostics, lead-season matrices, spatial maps, and observed-threshold event subsets. In the 2021--2023 matrix evaluation used here, \methodname{} reduces all-case CRPS by 32.2\% for precipitation and 34.0\% for 2 m temperature on the all-grid mask, with similar land-mask CRPS gains of 32.3\% and 32.7\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) prediction occupies the difficult interval between deterministic weather prediction and seasonal climate outlooks, sometimes described as a predictability valley. At this range, atmospheric initial conditions lose deterministic control while slower land, ocean, and tropical modes can still provide useful predictability \citep{Vitart2017,RobertsonVitart2018}. Forecast users nevertheless need calibrated probabilities rather than a single best estimate, especially for precipitation and temperature extremes whose societal consequences depend on tail risk. This motivates methods that can use physically based dynamical guidance while correcting systematic forecast errors and quantifying residual uncertainty.

\modelshort{} subseasonal forecasts provide a physically grounded ensemble prior, but, like other dynamical S2S systems, may contain flow-dependent bias, regional calibration errors, under- or overdispersion, and limited precipitation sharpness at longer leads. Statistical postprocessing and ensemble model output statistics have long been used to improve weather forecasts \citep{Gneiting2005,RaspLerch2018,Gronquist2021}, but gridpoint-oriented corrections can struggle to preserve spatial coherence and cross-variable dependence when used without an explicit spatial model. Recent machine-learning weather models show that learned global forecast mappings can be highly skillful \citep{Bi2023,Lam2023}, and stochastic neural forecasts increasingly emphasize ensemble distributions and proper scoring rules rather than deterministic trajectory error alone \citep{Lang2024}. However, for subseasonal impacts, a useful system should neither discard dynamical model information nor evaluate rare events only through an ensemble mean.

This paper frames the repository implementation as a probabilistic forecast-refinement system. \modelshort{} supplies the dynamical forecast state; observed ocean, land, circulation, moisture, and MJO-related predictors supply recent boundary and large-scale context; static geography supplies persistent spatial structure; and a flow-matching model learns an observation-consistent distribution over weekly precipitation and 2 m temperature. Flow matching trains continuous generative models by regressing velocity fields along probability paths \citep{Lipman2023,Liu2023}, making it a natural fit for stochastic ensemble generation when paired with climate-informed initial perturbations. In this setting, the ensemble noise is not a disposable implementation detail: it defines the starting distribution for every forecast member and therefore controls which spatial covariance structures the learned velocity field must refine.

The contributions are fourfold. First, we formulate a global 1 degree weekly S2S forecast-refinement task for precipitation and 2 m temperature conditioned on \modelshort{} ensemble summaries. Second, we implement a lead-aware multi-target flow-matching architecture with static geography, global-context cross-attention, separate precipitation and temperature heads, and structured velocity losses, reducing competition between short-lead synoptic corrections and longer-lead planetary-scale behavior. Third, we introduce a physically informed stochastic ensemble prior: EOF perturbations conditioned on MJO, NAO, ENSO, and lead week are blended with random noise and variance-tempered spread adjustment, and the production training path uses the same structured-noise family during training and inference to reduce prior mismatch. Fourth, we define an evaluation protocol that treats routine skill, stochastic-prior ablations, and high-impact extremes as related but distinct verification problems: all-case forecasts are evaluated with CRPS, RMSE, bias, correlation, and BSS; noise strategies are compared through variable-specific CRPS skill; and extremes are evaluated with observed-threshold tail probabilities and quantiles.

\section{Data and Forecast Problem}

\subsection{Prediction Task}

The prediction task is global weekly subseasonal forecasting on a $181 \times 360$ latitude-longitude grid. For each \modelshort{} initialization, the system forecasts four weekly leads. The target variables are weekly mean precipitation, verified against GPCP-derived precipitation \citep{Adler2003}, and weekly mean 2 m temperature, verified against ERA5-derived temperature \citep{Hersbach2020}. Precipitation is predicted in physical units after a square-root target transform. Temperature is represented during training as the residual between observed ERA5 2 m temperature and the target-lead \modelshort{} ensemble-mean 2 m temperature; the residual is added back to \modelshort{} during inference.

The repository configuration \texttt{ml\_model/config\_flow\_finalv1\_global.yaml} uses training years 1999--2020 and validation/evaluation years 2021--2023. The matrix evaluation script is designed for 2021--2023 forecast stores with lead weeks retained explicitly. Final manuscript results should be computed from a frozen checkpoint and a frozen generated-forecast archive before submission.

\subsection{Dynamical and Observed Predictors}

The model conditions on \modelname{} weekly ensemble information for precipitation and 2 m temperature. \modelname{} denotes the NOAA/ESRL FIMr1p1 forecast system in the S2S archive \citep{Vitart2017}, described there as a finite-volume atmospheric model coupled with ocean and land components. The FIM model family is documented as a flow-following finite-volume global model \citep{Lee2006FIM}, and NOAA's broader NWP modeling context is summarized by \citet{Benjamin2019NWP}. Rather than passing only an ensemble mean, the current global implementation stores five \modelshort{} summary statistics for each variable and lead: mean, standard deviation, 10th percentile, 90th percentile, and member count. By default, the training script uses the \modelshort{} statistics for the target lead only, which keeps the conditioning channel count compact and aligns the correction with the verification lead.

Observed predictors are grouped into local target-grid predictors and global context predictors. In the production global configuration, local predictors are soil moisture and an MJO wave field, while global context predictors are sea-surface temperature, sea-surface salinity, integrated vapor transport, 500 hPa geopotential-height zonal deviation, and 250 hPa zonal wind. These predictors are represented as four weekly pre-initialization fields. The preprocessing scripts indicate NOAA OISST-style SST input \citep{Reynolds2007}, Copernicus sea-surface salinity products \citep{CopernicusMarineSSS}, C3S/ESA-style soil moisture products \citep{Dorigo2017}, and ERA5-derived circulation and moisture fields \citep{Hersbach2020}; final source citations should be matched to the exact archived products used in the final experiment.

\subsection{Static Geography}

Static geography is included as a persistent model input. The implementation stores five channels: min-max normalized elevation, land mask, normalized latitude, sine longitude, and cosine longitude. The elevation source is configured as a GLDAS/GTOPO30-derived global elevation field. These fields allow the neural correction to depend on coastlines, orography, land-ocean contrast, and periodic longitude structure without relearning them from dynamic predictors.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Forecast Refinement View}

Let $y_{\ell}$ denote the observed weekly target field at lead week $\ell \in \{1,2,3,4\}$ and let $g_{\ell}$ denote the \modelshort{} ensemble information for the same lead. The goal is not to replace \modelshort{} with a free-running neural forecast, but to learn a conditional forecast distribution
\begin{equation}
  p_{\theta}(y_{\ell}\mid g_{\ell}, z, s, \ell),
\end{equation}
where $z$ contains recent observed predictors and $s$ contains static geography and calendar information. This framing is closest to distributional forecast refinement: the dynamical model provides a physically informed prior state, and machine learning learns spatially coherent, observation-consistent corrections.

\subsection{Conditional Flow Matching}

\methodname{} uses a conditional flow-matching model. Let $x_1$ be the normalized target field and $x_0$ be a stochastic initial field. For $t \in [0,1]$, the model is trained on the linear path
\begin{equation}
  x_t = t x_1 + (1-t)x_0,
\end{equation}
with target velocity
\begin{equation}
  v^\star = x_1 - x_0.
\end{equation}
The neural network predicts $v_{\theta}(x_t, c, t, \ell)$, where $c$ is the conditioning tensor. The base velocity objective is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vel}} =
  \frac{1}{N}\sum_i w_{\mathrm{area},i} w_{\mathrm{lead},i}
  \left\lVert v_{\theta}(x_t,c,t,\ell)_i - v^\star_i \right\rVert_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $w_{\mathrm{area}}$ is a latitude cosine area weight and $w_{\mathrm{lead}}$ increases from week 1 to week 4. The configured lead weights are $(1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0)$.

Forecast samples are generated by explicit Euler integration,
\begin{equation}
  x_{t+\Delta t} = x_t + \Delta t\,v_{\theta}(x_t,c,t,\ell),
\end{equation}
from $t=0$ to $t=1$. The global configuration uses 10 integration steps for validation and testing, with 15 validation ensemble members and 90 test ensemble members.

\subsection{Architecture}

The implemented model wraps a U-Net backbone \citep{Ronneberger2015} in a flow-matching formulation. The state channels are precipitation and 2 m temperature residual/target channels. Conditioning channels are concatenated with the flow state, and static geography is appended internally. A context MLP summarizes the conditioning tensor through spatial means and standard deviations; lead week is represented by a learned embedding; and a global context encoder maps coarse global predictor fields into spatial tokens. A bottleneck cross-attention module allows local U-Net bottleneck cells to attend to these global tokens.

The output stage contains separate lead-specific precipitation heads and temperature heads, plus separate variance heads for each variable and lead. This design shares most spatial representation learning while allowing week-1 and week-4 corrections to specialize. The intended benefit is to avoid capacity competition between filters that correct short-lead weather-scale errors and filters that represent slower week-3/week-4 circulation and teleconnection patterns. It also avoids forcing precipitation and temperature, which have very different spatial and distributional structure, through a single final projection.

\subsection{Target Transforms and Physical Bounds}

Precipitation is square-root transformed before normalization and decoded by squaring the predicted field, with a nonnegative clamp. This reduces the dynamic range of heavy precipitation while preventing negative precipitation at inference. Temperature is trained either as an absolute 200--320 K bounded field or, in the production global configuration, as a \modelshort{} residual bounded by statistics from the global stats file. In residual mode, the decoded residual is added to the target-lead \modelshort{} ensemble mean. These transformations are not conservation constraints, but they impose simple physical bounds that make generated samples more meteorologically plausible.

\subsection{Structured Velocity Loss}

The velocity loss is augmented with variable-specific spatial-structure terms. For each variable, the implementation computes a pixel loss, a first-gradient loss in latitude and longitude, and coarse losses after 2x and 4x average pooling. The total per-variable velocity loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{q} =
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pix},q}
  + \lambda_{\nabla,q}\mathcal{L}_{\nabla,q}
  + \lambda_{2,q}\mathcal{L}_{2,q}
  + \lambda_{4,q}\mathcal{L}_{4,q},
\end{equation}
for $q \in \{\mathrm{PR},\mathrm{T2M}\}$. The final structured loss averages the precipitation and temperature totals. This term encourages the learned velocity field to preserve both local gradients and broader spatial organization.

\subsection{Physically Informed Stochastic Prior}

The stochastic forecast distribution begins with an initial field $x_0$. In a conventional generative forecast, $x_0$ might be drawn from independent Gaussian noise. That choice is mathematically convenient, but it asks the learned velocity field to create all coherent precipitation and temperature structure during the ODE integration. For subseasonal prediction, this is an unnecessarily weak prior because large-scale modes already provide information about plausible spatial covariance. \methodname{} therefore treats the ensemble perturbation itself as a physical input to the forecast system.

The repository supports both Gaussian noise and EOF-based structured noise. The production configuration uses a mixture in which EOF perturbations are conditioned on MJO, NAO, ENSO, and lead-week state when the corresponding bases are available. The MJO is a known source of subseasonal tropical and extratropical predictability \citep{MaddenJulian1971,WheelerHendon2004}; NAO and ENSO states provide additional large-scale circulation and oceanic regime information \citep{WallaceGutzler1981,BarnstonLivezey1987}. EOF bases provide a compact way to represent recurring spatial modes \citep{Lorenz1956}. Latin-hypercube sampling is used within the EOF coefficient space so that a finite ensemble explores the structured perturbation distribution more evenly than repeated independent draws.

For precipitation and temperature, the implementation blends structured and random perturbations with separate coefficients:
\begin{equation}
  x_0^{(q)} =
  \rho_q x_{\mathrm{EOF}}^{(q)}
  + \sqrt{1-\rho_q^2} x_{\mathrm{rand}}^{(q)}.
\end{equation}
The training configuration starts with $\rho_{\mathrm{PR}}=0.15$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{T2M}}=0.05$, increasing after epoch 26 to 0.25 and 0.08, respectively. Validation uses the latter values. This deliberately gives precipitation a stronger structured prior than temperature, reflecting the greater importance of organized tropical convection, monsoon structure, and spatial displacement uncertainty for weekly precipitation.

Using structured noise only at inference would expose the ODE solver to initial states that the velocity model did not see during training. The global configuration therefore trains with the EOF-LHS mixture when available. This native structured-prior training is intended to make the learned flow closer to the sampling path used at evaluation time: the initial state already contains broad spatial covariance patterns, so the velocity field can focus on observation-conditioned refinement rather than constructing planetary-scale structure entirely from white noise. The key ablation is therefore not merely ``more ensemble members''; it is whether a physically informed prior improves CRPS and tail probabilities relative to a pure Gaussian prior at the same checkpoint, ensemble size, and ODE-step count.

\subsection{Variance-Tempered Spread Control}

The model includes variance heads that estimate a relative spread multiplier. The variance loss compares the predicted standard-deviation multiplier to a normalized absolute velocity residual and regularizes the multiplier toward a physically moderate range. During inference, the predicted multiplier is clipped, optionally coarse-grained, and tempered toward one:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1 + \beta_q(\sigma_{\theta}-1).
\end{equation}
The configured validation values are $\beta_{\mathrm{PR}}=0.45$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{T2M}}=0.03$, with an 8-grid-cell coarse variance kernel. This design lets the ensemble express heteroscedastic uncertainty without allowing the variance head to dominate the full flow trajectory.

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Training Configuration}

The production global configuration trains from scratch with batch size 4, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, BF16 mixed precision, and an 80-epoch horizon. The schedule is organized into velocity-only, joint velocity/variance, and variance-head phases: epochs 1--25 emphasize the velocity field, epochs 26--54 enable joint variance learning, and epochs 55--80 train variance heads only unless overridden. Training uses context dropout for non-\modelshort{} predictors, but \modelshort{} is kept active by default because it is the operational dynamical prior.

The flow-time sampler emphasizes low and middle interpolation times through the configured \texttt{stratified\_low\_mid} schedule. This choice increases exposure to the early denoising/refinement portion of the ODE trajectory, where stochastic initial conditions most strongly affect ensemble structure.

\subsection{Baselines}

The principal baseline is the raw \modelshort{} ensemble on the same variable, lead, initialization, and grid. For deterministic metrics, the \modelshort{} ensemble mean is compared with the \methodname{} ensemble mean. For probabilistic metrics, the full \modelshort{} ensemble and the generated neural ensemble are evaluated as empirical predictive distributions. This is the fairest baseline for the system because \modelshort{} is both a conditioning source and the operational dynamical forecast to be refined.

\subsection{Noise-Prior Ablation Protocol}

Because \methodname{} is a stochastic forecast generator, the ensemble initialization must be evaluated as a first-order algorithmic choice. The manuscript should compare at least three neural sampling strategies with the same checkpoint, data split, ensemble size, ODE solver, and conditioning fields. The first is a pure Gaussian prior, $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$, which tests the learned flow without physical covariance in the initial ensemble. The second is an EOF-LHS prior without variance tempering, which tests whether regime-conditioned spatial modes alone improve distributional quality. The third is the production physically informed prior, combining EOF-LHS perturbations with variance-tempered spread control.

The primary ablation score is CRPS skill relative to raw \modelshort{} for each target variable, mask, and lead. A secondary score is the change in CRPS relative to the pure-Gaussian neural ensemble, which isolates the value of the physically informed stochastic prior after the neural architecture and dynamical conditioning have been held fixed. The repository implementation for this comparison is \texttt{ml\_model/compare\_noise\_flow\_finalv1\_global.py}, which writes \texttt{noise\_comparison\_global\_<year>\_<sample>\_<checkpoint>.csv}. Final manuscript claims about the noise prior should be tied to that frozen ablation CSV, not inferred from the production run alone.

\subsection{All-Case Verification}

The primary all-case probabilistic metric is the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), a proper score widely used for ensemble forecasts \citep{MathesonWinkler1976,Hersbach2000,GneitingRaftery2007}. For an empirical ensemble $\{x_m\}_{m=1}^{M}$ and observation $y$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CRPS} =
  \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|x_m-y|
  - \frac{1}{2M^2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}|x_m-x_n|.
\end{equation}
The deterministic metrics are ensemble-mean RMSE, MAE, bias, and Pearson correlation. Spatial reductions use latitude cosine weights. Spread diagnostics should include the ensemble standard deviation and a spread-skill ratio, with rank-histogram or PIT-style checks added when the generated forecast archive contains enough ensemble members for stable distributional diagnostics.

\subsection{Spatial Skill Verification}

Lead-mean skill curves are necessary but not sufficient. The final evaluation should include spatial skill maps for each variable and lead so that improvements can be checked for coherent geographic structure rather than being inferred from domain averages alone. For all-case verification, the map suite should include \modelshort{} CRPS, \methodname{} CRPS, CRPS skill percentage, RMSE skill percentage, bias change, correlation change, ensemble spread, and spread-skill ratio. For probabilistic event verification, the map suite should include raw BSS, calibrated BSS, event frequency, and event-subset CRPS. These maps should be shown globally and over land-only masks, with seasonal or valid-month slices included where the all-case mean hides important behavior. For precipitation, neighborhood versions of event probability and BSS should also be reported to reduce unrealistic penalties from small spatial displacement at subseasonal lead.

\subsection{Event and Tail-Risk Verification}

High-impact extremes are evaluated as forecast-distribution problems rather than ensemble-mean reconstruction problems. The matrix evaluation suite defines events using observed climatological threshold maps, with the default event $y \geq q_{0.95}$ at each grid cell and a minimum precipitation threshold of 5 mm day$^{-1}$. For each event definition, the suite computes raw and calibrated Brier scores and Brier Skill Scores \citep{Brier1950,Murphy1973}. The calibrated BSS path uses leave-one-year-out logistic reliability calibration of raw exceedance probabilities. This separates two questions: whether the ensemble assigns high probability to observed extremes, and whether those probabilities are statistically reliable. The main text should include one temperature event and one precipitation event: the July 2022 UK heatwave, during which the UK record high temperature of 40.3 C at Coningsby was verified by the Met Office \citep{MetOffice2022Heatwave}, and the June 2022 northeastern Bangladesh/Sylhet-Sunamganj flood period associated with heavy monsoon rainfall and widespread flooding \citep{Reuters2022BangladeshFloods}.

Pure Gaussian sampling already improves substantially over raw \modelshort{}, but adding physically structured EOF-LHS perturbations with the higher-spread setting further improves CRPS. PR CRPS skill increases from 24.9\% under Gaussian sampling to 28.4\%, a 4.7\% relative CRPS reduction versus the Gaussian neural ensemble. T2M CRPS skill increases from 41.7\% to 43.2\%, a 2.6\% relative CRPS reduction versus Gaussian sampling. The expected scientific interpretation is not that EOF perturbations are merely a different random seed: a pure Gaussian prior asks the flow to synthesize spatial covariance from scratch, whereas the EOF-LHS prior starts each member on a low-dimensional manifold of historically observed, regime-consistent anomaly structures.

\section{Discussion}

The main scientific value of this framework is its hybrid interpretation. \modelshort{} supplies a dynamically consistent prior distribution, while the neural model learns a conditional transformation toward observations. This is different from a purely data-driven rollout and different from scalar bias correction. The network sees spatially complete \modelshort{} forecast summaries, recent observed environmental predictors, and static geography, and it produces a new ensemble distribution with spatial covariance inherited from both the learned flow and the physically informed stochastic prior.

Several design choices are likely important. The \modelshort{} residual target for temperature focuses learning on forecast error rather than the full seasonal temperature range. Static geography can help the model learn persistent biases associated with coastlines, elevation, and land-ocean contrast. Global-context cross-attention gives local grid cells access to large-scale predictors that are central to subseasonal predictability. Separate precipitation and temperature heads recognize that precipitation is intermittent and displacement-prone, while temperature is smoother and more strongly tied to large-scale circulation and surface properties. The physically informed prior addresses a different part of the problem: it improves the starting ensemble geometry before the neural ODE is integrated, giving the model plausible large-scale anomaly structures to refine instead of forcing every member to originate from unstructured noise.

The extreme-event evaluation must be interpreted carefully. At subseasonal leads, a large ensemble mean should be smoother than individual outcomes; that is a feature of probability forecasting, not a failure by itself. For rare events, the central verification question is whether the forecast distribution assigns elevated, spatially relevant probability to the event and whether the observed event falls within a plausible forecast tail. This is why the evaluation suite includes quantiles, exceedance probabilities, event masks, BSS, calibrated BSS, and event-subset CRPS.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

This manuscript is a methods-and-evaluation draft grounded in the current codebase and the supplied 2021--2023 matrix outputs. The most important remaining evaluation limitation is that the named-event catalog, quantile case-study products, regional post-processing CSVs, and uncertainty intervals are not yet inserted. A second limitation is that the structured stochastic prior is partly heuristic: EOF bases and teleconnection regimes are physically motivated, but their mixture weights are manually configured rather than learned end to end. Third, the variance mechanism adjusts the initial spread and does not inject stochasticity continuously along the ODE trajectory. Fourth, precipitation remains difficult at weeks 3--4 because heavy rainfall is intermittent, spatially displaced, and often connected to mesoscale or tropical-cyclone processes that are not deterministic targets at subseasonal lead.

Future work should freeze the data manifest and checkpoint set, add confidence intervals for the all-case and event-subset metrics, extend the pure-Gaussian versus physically informed prior comparison across additional validation years and event subsets, and insert the event-specific catalog and quantile diagnostics for the UK heatwave and Bangladesh/Sylhet flood case studies. The strongest next ablations are: no-\modelshort{} conditioning, no-static-geography, no-global-cross-attention, random-noise versus EOF-LHS initialization, variance tempering on/off, learned versus fixed EOF mixture weights, and temperature absolute-target versus \modelshort{}-residual training. Longer-term extensions could learn the stochastic prior directly, train with differentiable CRPS in physical units, add conservation-aware precipitation constraints, inject stochasticity continuously along the ODE path, and expand the target set to circulation variables.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a codebase-grounded manuscript draft for \methodname{}, a physically informed stochastic flow-matching framework for global probabilistic subseasonal forecast refinement. The system uses \modelshort{} ensemble guidance, recent observed predictors, static geography, lead-aware neural architecture, regime-conditioned EOF-LHS stochastic initialization, and variance-tempered spread control to generate ensemble forecasts of weekly precipitation and 2 m temperature. The inserted matrix evaluation shows broad all-case skill gains over \modelshort{} in both probabilistic and deterministic scores, with positive CRPS and RMSE skill across every season-lead cell for both target variables in the all-grid and land-mask evaluations.

The central message is that machine learning can be used as an observation-consistent distributional correction of a physics-based subseasonal ensemble, rather than as a replacement for the dynamical model, and that the stochastic prior used to generate ensemble members should itself contain physical information. The remaining result gaps are now narrower: controlled noise-prior ablations, named-event case-study metrics, regional post-processing summaries, confidence intervals, and final figure assets are still needed to fully support the ensemble-generation and high-impact tail-risk claims.
